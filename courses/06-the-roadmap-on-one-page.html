Learn Ship Playbook with Phoebe · Session 6 of 6

The roadmap on one page

Five sessions produced five separate outputs: a sorted list, a verdict per task, a bench result, a gate with named gaps, and a sentence about how the business makes its money. On their own they are five documents that will drift apart by the next planning round. Session 6 folds them into a single page that fits on a single page, dated, with the reasons attached to the decisions, and with a named section for what is deliberately not on the roadmap. That last section is the one that saves the most time, because it turns a quarterly argument into a line somebody can read.

Part 0

Where we end up

The spine is finished at the end of this session, and the thing it produces is not a plan. It is an artefact: one page, written down, that a vertical roadmap can import so the industry write-up spends its pages on the industry rather than re-deriving the same four moves. Without it, every vertical carries its own copy of the frame, and copies disagree. It is not a matter of whether, only when.

Part 1 · covers the artefact and its seven sections

One page, seven sections, nothing else

7 min live

The constraint is the point. A page that runs to four pages stops being read, and once it stops being read the decisions inside it stop being revisited. Seven sections, each short enough to argue with.

LiveWhy section 7 exists, and why it is named rather than implied4 min▶

Most roadmaps are a list of what is in. An item that was considered and rejected simply vanishes, which means the person who asked for it has no idea it was considered at all. So they ask again next quarter, in good faith, and the whole argument is re-run from nothing by people who were not in the first room.

Naming it fixes that cheaply. Section 7 lists each rejected candidate, in the words it arrived in, with one line on why. The important entry is the popular one: on the bench in session 3 the single most-requested item on the list needed no data at all, cleared every gate in 4 weeks, and carried a revenue fit of zero. It passes everything except the gate that matters. If that is not written down with its reason, it comes back, and it comes back with momentum.

The tone matters more than the content here. "Not on the roadmap because it does not move the money this business makes, and here is the money sentence" is a reason somebody can disagree with in a specific way. "Not prioritised" is not a reason, it is a way of ending a conversation without having it, and it guarantees the conversation repeats.

LiveWhat each section inherits, and what it must not restate3 min▶

Section 1 is one sentence. Not a business model canvas. From session 5: the same use case is worth different amounts at different businesses, so this sentence is what gives every score below it a meaning. If it takes two sentences, the second one is usually describing a different business inside the same company, and that is a finding worth its own page.

Sections 2 and 3 are one table, two columns. Rung and verdict. They are separate axes and putting them in one table is what stops them being conflated. A row can read "predict it / augment the person" and that combination is the normal case, not an edge case.

Section 4 is binary and short. Clears today, or does not, with the blocking entity named. No maybes. "Held partly" resolves to does not clear, which is the whole lesson of session 4.

Section 5 lists gaps, not wishes. Each one classified as a form change, a pipeline change or a standing job, because those three have wildly different costs and a list that does not distinguish them is a list nobody can price.

Section 6 is three items and three reasons. Not a twelve-quarter Gantt chart. Three, because that is roughly what anybody can hold, and the reasons are what make the three revisable.

Self-studyThe sequence, and how session 3 changed what belongs in it4 min▶

The instinct is to sequence everything and start at the top. The bench in session 3 put twelve candidates through the two gates and the result was blunt: dropping everything that does not clear the gate today took the plan from 64 weeks to 27, with revenue fit unchanged at 12, because the same six cases cleared in both versions. Two thirds of the plan length was work that could not be built and therefore contributed nothing while still consuming the estimate.

That is why section 6 only sequences what clears. The rest is not deleted, it moves into section 5 as a gap with a named blocker, which is a different kind of object: a gap is something you can choose to close, whereas a blocked item sitting in a sequence is a promise you cannot keep.

The other half of the same bench is the reason section 7 has to name the popular item. The three most-requested new-data cases produced a revenue fit of 0 over 25 weeks. Enthusiasm and revenue fit turned out to be uncorrelated on that board, and there is no reason to expect them to correlate on yours either, in either direction.

One honest limit, the same one every figure on these pages carries: the gym chain is invented, every effort figure is stipulated, and none of it is a claim about how often real portfolios are blocked or how often the favourite is empty. The arithmetic is real. The chain is not. The mechanism is what transfers.

Part 2 · covers surviving a planning cycle

What makes the page survive contact with a planning cycle

5 min live

A roadmap written once and never revisited is worse than no roadmap, because it carries the authority of a decision and the content of a guess. Three habits make the difference, and all three are about writing down the reason next to the thing.

Date it, and attach the reason to every decision. Not a version number, a date, at the top, next to the money sentence. A decision with its reason attached can be noticed going stale by anybody who reads it. A decision without one can only be defended or overturned, and both of those need the original room back.

LiveTriggers: what would change this line, written on the line3 min▶

Next to every gap, what would change its classification. A standing job that somebody finds a feed for becomes a pipeline change. A pipeline change with a screen already in the build queue becomes a form change. Writing the trigger turns a gap from a permanent complaint into something with a condition attached, and conditions can be met by people who were never in the planning meeting.

Next to every off-the-roadmap item, what would move it on. Usually one of two things: the money sentence changes, or a gate it failed gets cleared by work done for another reason. For the zero-fit favourite, the honest trigger is the first kind. It is not "we will get to it", it is "if the business starts making money in a way this item touches, it moves, and here is the sentence it would have to change".

And the discipline from session 5: do not quote a failure-rate statistic in the business case. Not one of the three circulating numbers survives inspection. One expired by its own stated terms, one traces back to an unattributed remark on a conference panel, and one is called misguided by a study that went and read its sources. The temptation is real, because a scary percentage makes a slide land. Resist it, because the case does not need it: the argument from the two gates is that two thirds of the plan length cannot be built at all, and that is a statement about your own board that anybody in the room can check.

Self-studyReading somebody else's roadmap with the same instrument4 min▶

The four moves work in reverse. Handed a roadmap you did not write, from a vendor, a consultancy, a previous post-holder or another division, run four questions down the items and stop. It takes about twenty minutes and it needs no access to anybody's systems.

What question does this item answer? Items that name a technology and a location answer none. From session 1, those belong in a "no question yet" pile rather than being assigned a rung to be polite, and on a document you have inherited that pile is usually the largest column. That is a finding about the document, not about you.

Which rung is it on? Then look at the rung below it and ask whether that one is trusted here. A skipped rung does not error, it ships and becomes confidently wrong, so an inherited roadmap with three rung-four items and a contested rung-one metric is a specific, sayable diagnosis.

What does it need that we do not hold? The fastest of the four. Most inherited roadmaps were written without anybody checking the entity list, and "held partly" is the state that kills, because the query runs and the answer looks like an answer.

Does it move the money this business makes? Last, because it is the one that requires the money sentence. This is where a plan built for a business with a different revenue model shows itself, which is the normal case for anything arriving from outside and is nobody's bad faith.

Two notes on manners. Running this on somebody else's document produces a lot of findings fast, and delivering all of them is a way of being right and useless. Lead with the rung diagnosis and the entity gaps, which are factual. And keep the verdict axis out of the first conversation entirely: from session 2, a verdict is passed on a task, never on a job or a department, and an inherited roadmap almost always states things at the level of a department.

Build-along

Write your page, then score it

★ 22 min · bring sessions 1 to 5

Five passes, in order, each using an output you already have. Then the scorecard below, which is the real test: not whether the page looks finished, but whether it carries the five things the spine was for.

Write the money sentence at the top, then date the page. One sentence on how this business makes its money, from session 5. Everything below is scored against it, so if it is wrong or vague nothing below means anything. The date is not decoration: it is what lets somebody in nine months know whether the reasons on the page were written before or after the thing that changed.

Write the sorted list with a verdict against each task. Rung from session 1, verdict from session 2, one table, two columns, so the two axes cannot be conflated. Pass the verdict on a task, never on a job or a department. Then count your augment verdicts: if there are none, that is worth a second look, because there is a published argument that the field carries excess incentives for automation over augmentation, so a portfolio with no augment verdicts is a signal about incentives rather than about the work.

Split the list into clears the gate today and does not, with the gap kinds. From sessions 3 and 4. Held partly resolves to does not clear, because that is the dangerous state: the query runs and the answer looks like an answer. For every blocked item, name the entity and classify the gap as a form change, a pipeline change or a standing job. On the bench, six of twelve entities were held in full, and the half-filled field was worth more than any new source.

Write the first three, and the reason each of the three is there. Only from what clears today. Three, not twelve. The reasons are what make the three revisable later, and they are the thing you will be asked for first. Remember what dropping the blocked items cost on the bench: the plan went from 64 weeks to 27 and the revenue fit stayed at 12, because the same six cases cleared either way.

Write what is deliberately off the roadmap, with what would move each item on, then score the card. Name the popular one explicitly. On the bench the most-requested item needed no data, cleared every gate in 4 weeks, and carried a revenue fit of zero. Next to each omission, the trigger that would move it on. Then take the six-row scorecard below and be harsh, because a 0 is the next thing to write rather than a mark against you.

If your page will not fit on a page. The commonest cause is section 2 carrying twenty candidates. That is not a formatting problem, it is the list not having been through the gate yet: run pass 3 and most of them move into section 5 as gaps, which is where they belong and where they are cheaper to argue about.

Score the whole spine

Six rows, one per session

10 min

One row for each of the six sessions. Score the page you just wrote, not the page you intend to write. The wording of each row is the claim from that session, so a low score points at a specific session to go back to.

Every candidate on my page sits on a named rung, and anything phrased as a technology is parked in a no question yet pile rather than assigned a rung to be polite. Session 1. The five categories are a dependency, not a maturity model: each rung consumes the one below it, so a broken lower rung does not error, it makes the top confidently wrong. The ladder applies per question, not per company, which is why a single page can honestly carry items at very different rungs.
Every item carries a verdict out of four, automate it, augment the person, build something new, or keep it human, and every verdict is passed on a task rather than on a job or a department. Session 2. The verdict is a separate axis from the category, and conflating the two is the commonest error in this work. There is a published argument that the field carries excess incentives for automation over augmentation, so a portfolio with no augment verdicts is a signal about incentives rather than about the work.
My sequence contains only what clears the gate today, and I can say what dropping the rest actually cost. Session 3. Reasons attached so that when one stops being true somebody can notice, triggers next to every gap and every omission, and the popular zero-fit item named by name: it needed no data, cleared every gate in 4 weeks, and carried a revenue fit of zero. Naming it has the argument once instead of every quarter.
Homework

After the course

45 min
  1. Show section 7 to the person who asked for the zero-fit item, before you show anybody the sequence. Not as a decision handed down, as a reason offered for disagreement. You will either keep the item off the page with their agreement or learn that the money sentence is wrong, and both of those are worth more than the meeting costs.
  2. Write the trigger next to your three most annoying gaps, including the ones you privately think will never close. A gap with a condition attached can be closed by somebody who was never in the room; a gap without one is just a complaint with a date on it.
  3. Take one roadmap you did not write and run the four questions down it. What question does each item answer, which rung, what does it need that you do not hold, and does it move the money this business makes. Stop there and write nothing else. Twenty minutes, and lead with the rung diagnosis, which is the factual part.
  4. Put a date in the calendar to re-read the page, one planning cycle out, with one instruction to your future self: check the reasons, not the items. Items going stale is normal and visible. A reason going stale is invisible, and it is what turns a good page into a page that is quietly wrong.
